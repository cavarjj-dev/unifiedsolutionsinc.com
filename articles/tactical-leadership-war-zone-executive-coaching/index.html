The Arc

What Tactical Team Leadership in a War Zone Taught Me About Executive Coaching

JJ
Julian Johnson ICF PCC · ACTC Candidate · Founder, Unified Solutions Inc.
| 7 min read | June 2026

It was Kosovo, the winter of 1999, and I was a 20-year-old Army communications specialist who had been handed more responsibility than I understood how to carry.

My job, on paper, was to maintain tactical communications for our unit. In practice, I was the person who had to make sure information moved accurately through a chain of command operating in an unstable peacekeeping environment, in the dark, under conditions that changed without notice. If I got it right, the right people knew what was happening. If I got it wrong, decisions got made on bad information. In that context, there was no ambiguity about the cost of either outcome.

I remember sitting with my team on an overnight watch shift, somewhere in the hills outside of Gnjilane, and watching one of my soldiers try to manage his fear by controlling everything in his immediate environment. Reorganizing gear. Double-checking equipment that was already checked. Moving without going anywhere. I recognized it because I was doing the same thing, with more authority and slightly better concealment.

What neither of us knew how to do yet was simply hold the uncertainty without trying to resolve it. That skill, as it turned out, would take me another two decades of leadership to actually develop. And it would take me working with senior executives in C-suite offices and university business school cohorts to fully understand why it was so hard to learn and so consequential not to.

The thing about pressure is that it does not reveal character, it accelerates it

This is the first thing I carry from that time into every coaching engagement. I have heard it said that pressure reveals character. I do not think that is quite right. Pressure does not reveal who you are. It accelerates who you already are becoming. The patterns that show up when the stakes are high were present before the stakes got high. They were just slower, easier to manage, easier to rationalize.

In a combat or near-combat environment, you see this clearly because the feedback loop is brutally short. A leader who manages anxiety by over-controlling their team does not get to find out six months later, in an annual review, that they have created a culture where people are afraid to surface problems. They find out in the next operational period, when the team stops telling them things.

In organizational life, the feedback loop is much longer. Eighteen months of suppressed information before a decision fails. Three years of managed-up culture before a high-potential leader leaves quietly. The pattern was there from the beginning. The pressure just made it visible eventually.

What this means for coaching is that when a senior leader comes to me with a presenting challenge, I am almost never working on the challenge itself. I am working on the pattern that produced it, and trying to do that work fast enough that they can see the pattern before it produces the next version of the same challenge.

You cannot build trust at the moment you need it

There is a principle in tactical operations that is so obvious it sounds like a cliche until you have watched someone violate it: you build trust before you need it. You do not attempt to establish the interpersonal conditions for effective collective action during the operation. You establish them in every low-stakes moment that precedes it. The quality of the relationships in a unit before deployment largely determines the quality of the decisions the unit makes under fire.

I have watched this play out, in reverse, in more executive teams than I can count.

A leadership team reaches a genuine organizational crisis, a merger that is not integrating, a major strategic pivot with real disagreement at the top, a leadership transition that nobody prepared for. And in that moment, the CEO discovers that the conditions for the honest conversation they need have never actually been built. People are professional. They are competent. They do not say what they actually think in the room. They have learned, over years of watching what happens when people do, that the cost is not worth it.

The leader usually does not know this until the moment when it matters most. And by then, you cannot build what you need in time to use it.

Psychological safety is not something you establish when you need it. It is something you build in every small moment that precedes the one where it will actually be tested.

In my coaching work with senior teams, I am often most useful not in the crisis conversation but in the hundred smaller conversations before it. The ones where a leader learns whether they can actually tolerate being wrong in front of their team, whether they can hold friction without resolving it prematurely, whether they can model the behavior they say they want without the exceptional conditions they think they need to do it. Those are the conditions that determine what is available to the team later, when everything costs something real.

Rank gets you compliance. Leadership gets you something else.

My experience in the Army was that authority and leadership were frequently confused, and that the confusion had predictable consequences. A non-commissioned officer who relied on rank to move people could produce compliance efficiently. He could not produce the initiative, the judgment, the willingness to improvise under ambiguous conditions that separated teams that were technically competent from teams that were genuinely effective.

This distinction matters more at higher organizational altitudes, not less.

The executives I work with are almost all in positions where pure authority is, theoretically, available to them. They can direct. They can require. They can remove. And yet the most consequential leadership they need to do, the kind that actually shapes organizational culture and builds the conditions for sustained performance, cannot be accomplished through the exercise of authority. It requires something more personal and more risky: genuine influence, built over time, through consistency between what they say and how they actually behave when it is inconvenient.

The leaders who confuse these two things tend to have teams that perform adequately in stable conditions and fragment under genuine pressure. Not because the team members are not capable. Because the conditions for collective trust and honest communication were never built. Authority was doing the work that only leadership can do, and authority has limits that leadership does not.

The loneliness is the job, not a symptom of doing it wrong

There is something that nobody prepares you for about operating at the top of any organization, military or otherwise. You carry things alone. Not because you have no one to talk to, but because there are things that need to be held at the leadership level rather than distributed through the organization before people are ready to process them.

In Kosovo, our commander knew things about the operational environment that would have destabilized the unit if shared without context and preparation. His job was not to withhold information dishonestly. His job was to hold organizational anxiety at the level where it could be managed, and to translate it into actionable clarity for the people who depended on him. That is a different kind of work than decision-making or strategic planning. It is more like a metabolic function. And it is exhausting in a way that is genuinely hard to describe to people who have not done it.

I see the same dynamic in almost every senior leader I coach. The specific shape of what they carry is different. But the experience of carrying it, alone, at the level where it lives, is recognizable across every sector, every industry, every organizational context I have worked in.

What I did not understand until I started coaching is that this loneliness is not a symptom of doing the job wrong. It is inherent to the altitude. The problem is not that it exists. The problem is that most organizations provide no legitimate structure for processing it. And what does not get processed tends to show up sideways, in the patterns and behaviors that eventually bring leaders to a coach.

What I did not know when I started this work

When I left the Army in 2003, I was twenty-four years old and carrying a set of experiences I did not have the language or distance to make sense of. I went on to spend the next decade building a consulting career inside Fortune 500 organizations, managing programs worth tens of millions of dollars, navigating the human complexity of large-scale transformations. And in all of that time, I was still learning, slowly and sometimes expensively, the same things that the hills outside of Gnjilane had first tried to teach me.

The through-line I can see now, looking back, is this: the hardest part of leadership at any level, in any context, is not the operational complexity. It is the personal development required to hold it. The self-awareness to recognize your own patterns before they cost someone else something. The relational capacity to build the conditions for honest collective action before the moment when you need them. The identity stability to carry what the role requires without becoming someone your values would not recognize.

This is what I work on with the leaders I coach. Not because it is interesting, though it is. Because it is the work. And because I spent enough years in contexts where the cost of not doing it was unmistakable.

The question I sit with, at the beginning of every new engagement, is the same one I could not have answered at twenty: what are you carrying right now that you have not yet found a way to look at directly? Not because looking at it will resolve it. Because you cannot lead well from a distance from yourself.

A note on this article: This is an experience piece, not a research article. The principles named here have research correlates in psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999), authentic leadership theory (Avolio & Gardner, 2005), and the developmental demands of senior leadership (Kegan, 1994). Those are explored in depth in other articles in this series. This one is meant to do something different: to tell you, as directly as I know how, where this coaching practice actually came from and what it is built on. The work is grounded in research. But the work also began in a place that no research paper has ever adequately captured.
