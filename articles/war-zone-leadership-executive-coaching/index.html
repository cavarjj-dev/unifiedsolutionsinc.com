What Tactical Team Leadership in a War Zone Taught Me About Executive Coaching

Baghdad International Airport, Iraq — 2003

In the spring of 2003, I was stationed at Baghdad International Airport, leading a team responsible for supporting the battalion Network Operations Center. The city outside the wire was not stable. The information environment inside it was not much better. We were working with systems that were not designed for what we were being asked to do, in a context that was changing faster than our briefings could track, under a chain of command that was itself making decisions with incomplete pictures.

I was twenty-something years old, accountable for a team of people who were watching me closely, and operating in conditions where the cost of a bad call was not abstract.

What I did not fully appreciate at the time, and have spent the years since working to understand, is that the hardest part of that job had almost nothing to do with technical competence. I knew communications systems. My team knew their roles. What none of us had been explicitly prepared for was the relational and cognitive load of leading through sustained ambiguity. The gap between what we knew and what we needed to act on was constant. How I occupied that gap mattered as much as any decision I made inside it.

What the NOC taught me about signal and noise

Running support for a battalion NOC means you are, in practice, managing information flow under pressure. You are triaging what is operationally relevant from what is noise. You are translating between technical realities and command needs. And you are doing all of this while your team is watching how you handle the moments when the answer is not clear.

I noticed something early in that deployment. The leaders who struggled were not struggling with the technical problem. They had access to the same systems, the same feeds, the same picture I did. What they struggled with was the space between receiving information and acting on it. In that space, something happened to them. Some contracted and waited for clarity that was not coming. Others moved too fast and created a different category of problem.

I am not certain I handled that space consistently well. There were days I got it right and days I did not. But I started to notice that how I occupied that space mattered more to the people around me than the quality of the decision I eventually made. They were not primarily watching my conclusions. They were watching my process. They were calibrating their own response to the situation against mine.

The texture of the problem does not change as much as we expect

Years later, sitting across from a VP of Operations navigating a major systems migration while absorbing a second round of team restructuring, I recognized something. Not the industry. Not the technology. Not the specific stakeholders. What I recognized was the texture of the problem.

Too much information to process cleanly. Decision timelines that did not match the pace at which clarity was arriving. A team watching to see whether the person at the front was going to hold.

The circumstances were entirely different. The stakes were different in the ways that matter most. I want to be careful here, because the comparison between military environments and organizational ones tends to flatten both and serve neither. What I am pointing at is something more specific: the cognitive and relational demands placed on a leader operating with incomplete information, inside a system watching them closely, making consequential decisions on timelines that do not allow for certainty. Those demands have a common structure across contexts. And that structure is something I had learned to recognize from a different starting point than most of the leaders I was working with.

What complexity actually requires of a leader

In Baghdad, I learned something about what complexity requires that I have not found in any leadership framework since. It requires the capacity to remain present and oriented when you do not have the information you would need to feel confident. It requires the ability to hold your team's sense of direction without pretending you have more clarity than you do. And it requires a quality of self-awareness that most high performers are never asked to develop, because their environments have historically rewarded execution over reflection.

Senior leaders face a version of this constantly. A board that wants a definitive forecast when the variables are still moving. A team that needs a decision before the analysis is complete. A strategic shift that requires communicating conviction while privately holding significant uncertainty. The technical skill to navigate these situations is rarely the limiting factor. What limits leaders is usually something more interior: their relationship to not knowing, and what they do with that discomfort before it becomes visible to the people around them.

What I think coaching is actually doing when it works

I spent a long time after leaving the Army trying to understand what the coaching relationship was actually doing when it worked. I have a cleaner answer now than I did then, though I hold it with some looseness.

Coaching, at its best, creates the conditions for a leader to examine the space between information and decision. Not to fill that space with better frameworks or faster processing. To slow it down enough that the leader can see what is actually happening in it. What assumptions are running. What they are afraid of. What they are certain about that may not deserve the certainty they have given it. What questions they have quietly stopped asking because the answers felt too uncomfortable or too inconclusive.

In Baghdad, I did not have anyone helping me do that examination in real time. I was doing it alone, imperfectly, often after the fact. What I needed and did not have was a structured way to interrogate my own process while I was still inside it. That is what I try to provide now. Not answers. A structure for finding the right questions.

What I am still uncertain about

I want to be direct about what I do not fully know, because I think the uncertainty is relevant.

I do not know whether my particular history gives me something that another coach with a different kind of experience could not provide. I am skeptical of the idea that combat experience is uniquely qualifying for anything outside combat. What I think is more honest is this: operating inside high-stakes ambiguity at a formative stage of my career shaped how I read a room, how I listen for what is not being said, and my ability to sit with a leader who is in a genuinely difficult place without needing to resolve it for them. Whether that came specifically from BIAP in 2003 or from the years of reflection that followed, I am not entirely sure.

I also do not know how far the connection I am drawing here generalizes. I have seen it hold across a wide range of leaders and industries. I have also worked with leaders for whom the framing was not useful, where the specificity of their context made the parallel feel forced rather than clarifying.

What I keep returning to is simpler than any framework. The leaders who make the most progress in this work are not the ones who get better information or cleaner models. They are the ones who get more honest with themselves about what is actually hard, and who develop the capacity to stay curious inside complexity rather than retreating to what they already know. I learned something about that capacity in Baghdad. I am still learning what it means.
